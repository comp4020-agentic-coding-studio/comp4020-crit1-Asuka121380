The Review

The first play was a relief. Nevermind had become so large that I feared Nirvana would either repeat it or collapse beneath the reaction. Instead, In Utero sounded abrasive, intimate, and deliberately uncomfortable. I bought it on the day it reached our local shop, then wondered whether my speakers were damaged during “Scentless Apprentice.” They were not. “All Apologies” is the track I return to most because its circular guitar figure offers peace without pretending the tension has vanished. “Heart-Shaped Box” is the perfect doorway into the album, while “Milk It” still makes me check the volume before other people enter the room. The ugliness can be tiring, and I do not always want to inhabit these songs from beginning to end. That seems an honest limitation rather than a flaw. After Kurt Cobain died, the record became harder to hear casually, but I resist treating every line as a prediction. It was alive before it became evidence.
